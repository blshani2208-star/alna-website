How ALNA
works.

ALNA identifies what is happening, examines what may be driving it, and helps the organization choose and implement a response worth testing.

1

Identifying the finding

We connect existing information with what frontline teams and management are seeing. The finding shows what is happening. It does not yet explain why.

2

Examining plausible drivers

We test several explanations, such as workload, capacity, unclear ownership, handoffs between teams, duplicated work, or the workflow itself.

3

Choosing and implementing a response

We compare feasible responses by fit, evidence, organizational capacity, and what can be tested in practice. How this works in practice →

4

Reviewing and learning

We review whether the response was implemented, what changed in the process or outcome, and what the organization should retain, adjust, or test next.

The platform organizes evidence, patterns, and response options. The analyst works with the organization to interpret them, choose a feasible action, and plan how it will be implemented and reviewed.

From evidence to action

The platform brings the evidence, competing explanations, and response options into one structured view. The analyst works with the organization to interpret that picture, choose a feasible response, translate it into practical steps, and review implementation at agreed points.

Professional judgment and the final decision remain with the organization. ALNA supports that work and records what was chosen, implemented, and learned over time.

Example: long wait times after intake.

  • WHAT WE SEE People wait a long time between first contact and the next stage of support.
  • WHAT COULD EXPLAIN IT Not necessarily a staffing shortage. It could also be an unsuitable division of work, unclear ownership, a disorganized handoff between teams, duplication, a cumbersome process, or difficulty tracking cases.
  • HOW ALNA TELLS THEM APART By connecting operational data with frontline and leadership perspectives, then checking which explanations are best supported and what remains uncertain.
  • WHAT THE RECOMMENDATION MIGHT BE It depends on what's found: sometimes redistributing responsibility, sometimes simplifying a process, and sometimes improving the handoff between teams.

Yes. The diagnosis is the foundation, not the end point. Implementation can be technological (tools, automation, decision-support systems) but just as often human: process change, redistributing responsibility, staff training, or adjusting how work gets done. What sets ALNA apart is that implementation is chosen based on what creates real change in the organization, not a predefined technology solution.

After the diagnosis, we choose together one or two points worth changing. As needed, ALNA and human support help turn the recommendation into a change that can actually be implemented. We then check back on what changed and what can be learned going forward.

ALNA's tracking layer continues to evolve. Over time, the goal is for it to rely as much as possible on information the organization and its existing systems already generate, so it doesn't create additional work for the team.

Cost is determined by the scope of the organization, the process selected for review, the number of roles involved, and the information sources included in the diagnosis. After an introductory call, we'll define the scope of work together and you'll receive a clear quote upfront. The diagnosis is priced as a defined project. After findings are presented, an action plan is built, and the scope of implementation support is set accordingly. You can start with a focused process and expand it only when there's a real need and value for the organization.

ALNA focuses on diagnosing organizational processes and does not require identifying information about clients or service recipients. The work is based on a data-minimization principle, and for every process, what information is needed and how it is handled is defined in advance in line with the organization's own procedures. The public website does not include user accounts or a user database. Contact details are kept only when provided at the visitor's own initiative.

The length of the process varies with the size and complexity of the organization, but it typically includes a scoping meeting, questionnaires, focused interviews, analysis of existing data, and a findings presentation meeting.

ALNA includes organizational analysis and is designed as a continuing learning process. It focuses on complex service pathways, connects data with day-to-day practice, examines what may be driving the gaps, and helps the organization choose an action and review what happened afterward. Professional consulting or broader organizational change can work alongside the platform where needed.

So the learning doesn't disappear after the diagnosis. The goal is to preserve, over time, the picture of the process, the mechanisms that were tested, the changes that were made, and what happened as a result, so the organization doesn't start from scratch every time.
