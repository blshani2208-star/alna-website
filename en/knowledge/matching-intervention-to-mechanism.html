SERVICE DIAGNOSTIC AND SYSTEM UNDERSTANDING

Same Problem, Different Causes, Different Actions

How to examine a change against the possible mechanism, the evidence, and the organization's capacity

ALNA Team · Updated September 2026

A similar outcome does not prove a similar cause

When a program lead sees the same issue recurring, such as follow-ups that are not completed, it is easy to reach for a familiar solution: another reminder, another form, another team meeting. The same observed problem can arise from very different mechanisms, and each mechanism points to a different action. An intervention that fits one explanation may miss another entirely. It may even add work where workload was never the main issue.

Illustrative scenario: incomplete follow-ups

In a service organization, one in four follow-ups scheduled after an intake meeting was not completed on time. The finding is clear. The explanation remains open.

Four plausible mechanisms

1. Capacity: the follow-up coordinator has more cases than can be completed within the expected time.

2. Ownership: no one is clearly responsible for confirming that the follow-up took place.

3. Feedback: there is no simple way to spot a delayed follow-up before it becomes significantly overdue.

4. Tools: follow-ups are recorded in several places, making it difficult to see who is waiting for what.

Different mechanisms call for different options

Each mechanism usually has more than one possible action. The examples below show a decision space with no preselected recommendation.

If capacity is the likely mechanism

Reprioritize work, redistribute capacity, or remove steps that do not add value to the follow-up process.

If ownership is unclear

Name one accountable role, remove duplicated responsibility, clarify the intake record, and create one confirmation point.

If feedback is missing

Use a simple status view or a targeted reminder that appears only when a follow-up is approaching its due date.

If the tools are fragmented

Bring documentation into one view or introduce a focused technical adjustment. A new system may not be necessary.

Options can be compared against:

• fit with the mechanism

• strength of evidence

• organizational feasibility

• additional staff burden

• resources and time

• compatibility with existing systems

• risks and unintended effects

• ability to measure implementation and outcome

Check the evidence before deciding

Before selecting an option, compare it with evidence that may already be available. Do coordinators who report the highest workload also have the highest rate of incomplete follow-ups? When asked directly, do frontline teams describe uncertainty about ownership? Evidence that repeatedly points in one direction gives the organization a stronger basis for choosing an action. It reduces uncertainty without pretending to eliminate it. ALNA and the analyst examine that evidence with the organization. They do not make the decision on its behalf.

Turn the selected option into a measurable test

Choose one option and define in advance what change would be meaningful. That might be a reduction in incomplete follow-ups over one month. Then set a time-limited implementation period and check three separate questions: Was the action carried out? Did the relevant behavior or process change? Does the original mechanism hypothesis still fit what was observed?

This scenario is illustrative. It does not describe a real organization.
